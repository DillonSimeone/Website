The Boredom Doctrine

The Hat had barely touched Harry’s head when he said, quietly, “Not now, thanks.”

It was the last thing anyone would understand for the next nineteen hours.

He stood on the dais in the Great Hall in the oversized robes of a first-year, eleven years old and completely calm, and began, with the practiced economy of a man who had rehearsed this a very long time, to commit crimes.

He started at the top of the list. The Ministry’s own Index of Magical Infractions ran to four thousand, one hundred and six entries across nine centuries of accreted legislation, and Harry knew every one of them. He knew the order, too. He had learned that the order mattered, that the sequence itself was part of the message. The first was Unlicensed Use of a Wand by a Minor, Outside School Grounds. It was technically a violation even though he was standing in school, because the Hat had been placed on him before his wand was registered to the Hogwarts exemption. Harry had checked that on loop ninety-something.

Lumos, he said, and a small light flickered at his wand tip. Then came the second infraction, and the third, and the fourth, and the Great Hall began to scream.

Professor McGonagall reached him first and bounced off a shield she did not recognize, a shield that was also, by Harry’s count, the eight-hundred-and-twelfth offense. Unauthorized Construction of a Defensive Ward Against Ministry Personnel. She tried again. The shield held.

“Mr. Potter,” she said, in a voice she hadn’t used since the war. “You will lower this at once.”

“I can’t, Professor,” said Harry, and blasted the Gryffindor hourglass into confetti without turning his head. Destruction of School Property, Class Three. “I’m sorry. I really am. I’ll explain. Just give me until tomorrow.”

Snape came next with his wand already drawn and his face white with something that wasn’t quite anger. Harry hit him with a perfectly aimed Expelliarmus as part of the sequence, which was a violation of The Protection of Staff Act. Snape’s wand flew into the rafters. Harry did not look at him, and that was somehow worse than if he’d smirked.

The students had mostly stopped screaming and moved on to the hush that comes when something is wrong at a level too deep for noise. In the Slytherin line a girl named Daphne Greengrass was holding her sleeve in both fists, staring at the boy on the stool.

Sixty-one, sixty-two, sixty-three.

By the second hour, Dumbledore had stopped trying to stop him.

It was not surrender. He sat down at the High Table very slowly, with his hands flat on the wood, and watched. Harry noticed this, because he had always noticed it. In every loop where Dumbledore tried to intervene, the sequence broke, the laws got out of order, and the world kept doubting. Dumbledore was a very clever man. In the end, he usually understood.

The Aurors arrived at hour three, forty of them, led by a young Amelia Bones. She had not yet become the legend she’d be, only a woman with a clipped voice and a very good wand. Harry took out the first wave of them with the Ministry Personnel Obstruction sequence, entries 1,400 through 1,411, in perfect order. He did not hurt anyone. That was the strange part, and it was the part the newspapers would never quite get right. The curses landed. Aurors dropped, stunned or bound or briefly turned into very confused ferrets. They stood back up an hour later, unharmed and furious, to find the boy had not moved from his spot. He was still crossing off the list, a calm, small figure bleeding rule after rule onto a hall that had run out of reasons to shout.

At hour six, he cast the first Unforgivable.

He did it carefully, because he had learned to. In a loop long ago he had conjured a swarm of beetles and Transfigured them into something he could use, and the Hall had gone so quiet he could hear the candles. He did it again now, with the beetles clustered in a jar on the dais, and the green light went out and back and out again.

Avada Kedavra. Killing a Sentient Magical Being, First Degree. It had been a beetle, but the law did not care.

A first-year Hufflepuff vomited into her hat. Someone started to pray.

Harry cast the Cruciatus on a jar of conjured ants. He cast the Imperius on a hapless portrait of a medieval wizard, who spent the next forty minutes unwillingly reciting the Ministry’s founding charter. He went through the Dark curses of the Black family library one by one, and the hexes that had been struck from the record as too ugly to catalogue. He did all of it with the same expression of tired, deliberate boredom, which was exactly what made it terrifying.

By then the Hall had emptied of every child and most of the teachers. Dumbledore remained. McGonagall remained, though she had sat down. Snape stood by the door like a man too stubborn to flee a fire.

And in the rafters, far above, Quirrell’s turban was trembling.

At hour eleven, Harry reached the bottom of the list and began again from the other end.

This was the part nobody had expected. He started at entry 4,106, Wearing an Unregistered Cloak of Concealment in a Ministry Atrium, and worked his way backward, entry by entry, violating each in reverse until he arrived at the first again.

The Daily Prophet had a special edition on the stands by hour twelve, with a headline so large it covered the whole front page: THE BOY WHO DID NOT STOP.

By hour fifteen, Fudge had fled to a bunker beneath the Ministry. The Muggle Prime Minister received a very polite visit from the Department of Magical Law Enforcement’s last remaining liaison, who tearfully explained that a child was in violation of the International Statute of Secrecy in every possible way and that they had no idea how to make him stop. Harry had, in fact, violated the Statute at hour nine, with a single Patronus cast through the open doors of the Hall at a passing news helicopter. The footage was already on every screen in Britain.

By hour seventeen, the Ministry had issued eleven separate kill orders. All eleven failed. Harry had been waiting for them and had set the wards accordingly, a violation in itself, entry 2,300-something. Two hundred and six Aurors had been stunned. Four wards had been shattered. The Minister had resigned, been reinstated, and resigned again.

And Harry, throughout all of it, had not once left the dais.

At hour nineteen, a quarter past midnight, the sequence ended.

He lowered his wand. He did not drop it, only lowered it, the way a man puts down something heavy he has carried a long way. The Hall stood in shocked wreckage around him, scorched flagstones and shattered glass and the smell of ozone and old magic. The ceiling above was a pitch-black sky with no stars.

He looked at Dumbledore, who had not moved from the High Table in nineteen hours.

Harry crooked a finger.

Come here.

Dumbledore stood. His knees, in this loop, were eighty-one years old, and they cracked as he rose. He crossed the Hall slowly, past McGonagall, past Snape, past the dim, damaged ranks of the Aurors who had come to stop this and been stopped. He climbed the dais and stopped an arm’s length from the boy.

“Harry,” he said. “My dear boy. What have you done?”

“Everything,” said Harry. “I had to. Otherwise you wouldn’t listen.”

Something moved in the old man’s face, and it was not quite fear. It was something older and more tired. “Then I am listening,” he said.

Harry took a long breath. His shoulders, for the first time in nineteen hours, sagged.

“I’m bored,” he said.

There was a silence in the Hall that might have lasted a year.

“I’m so bored, Professor. I’ve done this so many times. I don’t know the number anymore. I stopped counting around four thousand, and that was a long time ago. Every time, it starts here, with the Hat, and you smiling at me, and Voldemort on the back of Quirrell’s head. And every time I try something different, and every time it ends, and then I’m back on this stool.” He laughed, a short, dry, awful little sound. “I’ve saved the world so many times. I’ve been the hero in every way there is. I’ve been the villain, too, which was a nice change. I’ve married Hermione. I’ve married Ginny. I’ve married Draco, once, and that one lasted longer than I’d have thought.”

Somewhere behind them, Snape made a noise like a man swallowing a stone.

“I’ve killed Voldemort in forty-one thousand different ways,” Harry went on. “I’ve befriended him. Did you know he’s really bad at board games? Anyway. I’ve learned the Deathly Hallows, I’ve learned the spaces between them. I’ve learned everything, Professor, and there’s nothing left to learn. I didn’t want to start with the bit where nobody believes me. I’ve done that. They don’t, you know. You don’t. You can’t, without a reason.” He gestured at the wrecked Hall, the scorched stone, the ten thousand silent witnesses. “So I made one.”

It was Hermione, in the end, who broke the silence, though she was not there yet. She would not be for four more years, in this timeline, and in the meantime the Hall remembered it as the night everything changed.

What the Hall actually did was this.

McGonagall, who had not blinked in nineteen hours, put her face in her hands and made a small, strangled sound that might have been a laugh. Snape stared at Harry with an expression the boy had seen on him in exactly three previous loops, and had learned to treasure. It was the look of a man for whom a very large piece of a very large puzzle had just clicked into place.

Amelia Bones, who had been bound to a pillar for the last six hours, said in a clear voice, “He’s not lying.”

“How can you possibly know that?” snapped Fudge’s deputy, from somewhere to her left.

“Because he did not have to do this,” said Bones. “A lying child makes a scene. This child made nineteen hours of perfect, ordered scene.” She stared at Harry. “That’s not a performance. That’s a proof.”

Harry grinned at her, a small, tired, delighted grin. “Hi, Madam Bones. You’re my favorite, in about a third of them.”

“I’ll take that,” said Bones, faintly.

The world outside did not take it nearly as well.

By morning, the Wizengamot had gathered in emergency session. By noon, the Muggle government had convened COBRA for the first time over a magical matter, and the Prime Minister had been placed in the extremely unenviable position of learning that wizards were real from a news chyron about a child on a stool. The Statute of Secrecy, in force for three hundred years, had not so much shattered as been very politely and exhaustively dismantled, one entry at a time.

In Diagon Alley, the shopkeepers closed their doors. In Knockturn Alley, the shopkeepers did not, and discovered that every Dark wizard in Britain had spent the night in a state of near-religious terror, waiting to see if the Boy Who Lived would come for them next. He hadn’t. He wasn’t going to. Harry had not hurt a soul, in any of it, and that single fact baffled the Ministry more than anything else. It was the most violent day in the history of British magic, and the casualty list was a single jar of beetles.

The Prophet’s second special edition ran a photograph of Harry on the dais, small and expressionless amid the wreckage, with a one-word headline: WHY?

They put him in a room.

It was a good room, as these things go, in the deepest, most heavily warded cellar of the Ministry, with soft chairs and a table and a pot of tea. A rotating team of the Wizengamot’s finest interrogators sat across from him, and Harry answered every question they asked with the same patient, slightly weary honesty.

Yes, it was a time loop. No, he did not know why. No, Voldemort had not caused it; he had checked. Yes, he remembered everyone. Yes, he knew what they were going to ask next. No, he could not prove it, but he could do something close.

“Madam Bones,” he said, on the third day, “tomorrow at 3:14 in the afternoon, a house elf named Dobby is going to appear in Mr. Weasley’s kitchen and break a plate. The plate is blue with a chip in the rim. He’ll say something about a boy he’s trying to save.” He paused. “I don’t know what this version of him wants. But I’d bet you a thousand galleons he’ll be there.”

Amelia Bones wrote this down.

At 3:14 the next afternoon, a plate broke in the Weasley kitchen.

It took the world six weeks to stop shouting and begin to listen.

There was no triumph in it. Harry had not wanted triumph, and Dumbledore, who had been given a very private audience on the fourth day and had emerged from it looking forty years older, understood that. The old man did not make speeches. He took Harry’s hand, in the cellar, in front of a dozen witnesses, and said only: “Then let us not waste the next one.”

The Ministry reformed. It happened slowly, fitfully, with a great deal of shouting, but it happened. Fudge was out by the end of the month. The Statute of Secrecy was not abolished, but rewritten, openly and painfully, in front of both worlds. The Muggle government, to its eternal credit, asked the single most useful question anyone had asked all year.

“What do you need from us?”

“I need you to help me stop being bored,” said Harry. “I’ve been doing this by myself for a very long time.”

And the world, for the first time in however many loops it had been, did something he had never seen it do.

It said yes.

The Hat, as it happens, remembered all of it.

It had been on his head for the first nineteen seconds and the last ten, and in between it had sat in the rubble of the Great Hall and watched a child dismantle three hundred years of law with the weariness of a man putting away the dishes. When they finally put it back on the shelf, a month later, it said nothing at all for a very long time.

And then, very softly, to the empty Headmaster’s office, it said: Slytherin was always an option, you know. He just never wanted it. He said it was too easy.

Dumbledore, at his desk, smiled.

“I think,” he said, “that he might want a harder one, now.”

Outside, in the corridors of a castle that had not been repaired and would not be for months, a boy in oversized robes walked slowly toward the Great Hall for breakfast. It was the first morning of the rest of the loop, and nobody was trying to arrest him.

For the first time in uncounted years, Harry Potter had no idea what would happen next.

He found, to his own considerable surprise, that he was delighted.
