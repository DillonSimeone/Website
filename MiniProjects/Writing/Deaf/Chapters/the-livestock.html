The Livestock

Eli · The Specific Wound · One Wire Cut · Shame at the Shame

Scroll to read

The brown paper parcel had been delivered to Eli’s apartment on Thursday evening, tucked behind the bronze mail chute with his surname penciled in grease marker on the wrapper. Inside was a bound copy of the Commission on the Welfare of the Mute, a special investigative report printed in limited run by the State Legislative Assembly two winters prior. It had not been reviewed in the metropolitan newspapers, nor had Henderson mentioned it during any executive briefing, though the distribution warehouses covered in Section IV were located only nine miles south of Eli’s office.

Eli sat at his kitchen table under the hum of an unshielded yellow bulb, turning the dense rag pages with careful thumbs. The language of the testimonies was bureaucratic, drained of theatrical indignation, which only made its contours more jagged. It recorded thirty years of institutional conditions at the Hartford and Northampton asylums: the strapping of children’s wrists to oak chair arms during dining hours; the cold-water troughs behind the dormitories; the punitive withholding of food from pupils caught signing in the latrines; the systematic classification of unvocalized deaf adults under statutes governing agricultural property and domestic livestock.

It was not merely neglect. It had a specific, distinct flavor that did not attach itself to other infirmities.

Eli set his spectacles on the open ledger and rubbed the bridge of his nose. The comparison was laid out in an addendum by a dissident doctor named Albright, whose dissenting opinion occupied three densely set appendices. Albright’s argument was austere, mathematical, and entirely free of self-pity.

A man in a wheelchair moved differently through municipal corridors, but the primary conduit of civic life—the spoken command, the courtroom verdict, the casual joke exchanged over a counter—reached him uninterrupted. A ramp was missing, but the wire was live. A blind man navigated with cane and attendant, but he could sit in a hearing committee, track the subtle hesitations in a senator’s voice, and reply in the shared currency of breath and tone. A missing limb was a mechanical deficit. It slowed the laborer, but it did not exile him from the common mind.

Only deafness severed the primary channel. Not a missing ramp. The entire wire, cut clean to the brick.

The gap was not gradual; it was vertical. Because the hearing world was built upon speech, the inability to participate in speech was treated not as an injury, but as an intolerable vacancy—an animal silence that provoked in the hearing observer a deep, irrational disgust. To refuse to speak, or to sign in public with the fluid, rhythmic motions of the hands, was to confront hearing society with an alternative human architecture that it could neither enter nor control. And so they tied the hands down. They forced open the jaws. They called it education.

The response of the civic press to the report’s leaked findings had been instantaneous and impeccably civilized. Eli had read the editorials in the library at lunchtime. A Lamentable Legacy, the leading evening paper had titled its opinion piece. The board noted with somber satisfaction that the methods described belonged to “a different era,” that modern acoustic pedagogies and oral clinics had replaced the stern disciplines of the past, and that continued agitation from radical deaf separatist factions only risked undermining the public goodwill so generously extended by taxpayers.

Defensiveness masquerading as historical perspective. The comfortable assurance that because children were no longer beaten with leather straps for moving their fingers, the injury had ceased to exist.

At six o’clock, Eli stood on the corner of Elm and Lexington, waiting for the crosstown trolley beneath a wet canvas awning. The autumn drizzle had turned the slate sidewalks into dark mirrors reflecting the neon signage of butcher shops and dry cleaners. The corner was crowded with homeward-bound clerks, their coat collars turned up, their breaths fogging the cold air.

Across the intersection, near the mouth of the subway stairs, three teenagers in heavy wool caps were standing in a loose semicircle around an older man in an oilskin jacket. The older man was deaf. He had a bundle of evening newspapers under his arm, and with his free left hand he was attempting to explain something to a customer—a rapid, fluid movement that involved two fingers flicking against his collarbone and a question tilted in his eyebrows.

The three youths had caught the gesture. One of them, a tall boy with a cigarette pinched between his teeth, threw his head back in exaggerated parody, opening his mouth wide and slapping his chest with limp, flailing wrists, producing a series of loud, barking grunts that carried across the wet pavement.

The other two boys doubled over, hooting with delight. Commuters passed on either side of the tableau, glancing briefly with polite, nervous smiles before turning their faces away into their umbrellas. Nobody intervened. The older man froze. His face went flat, rigid as slate, the hand tucked instantly against his chest as if he had been struck with a cane.

◈ ◈ ◈

Eli stood thirty paces away under the awning, his feet locked to the concrete.

Every muscle in his abdomen coiled tight. He could have stepped into the crosswalk. He could have crossed the asphalt in eight strides, planted his frame between the boys and the vendor, and spoken with the crisp, middle-class accent his parents had spent twenty thousand dollars to construct. He could have used their language—the sharp, authoritative cadences of an office supervisor—to shame the youths into dispersing.

He did nothing.

He stood motionless, his hands stuffed into the pockets of his mackintosh, his chin pulled down into his woolen scarf. He did not look at the boys. He did not look at the vendor. He stared fixedly at the iron wheel of a parked laundry truck, praying with every cell in his body that the trolley would arrive before anyone on the corner looked toward him, before anyone noticed that he had not laughed, before anyone could ask why his jaw was clenched so hard the molars ached.

The particular paralysis of someone who lives in both houses and belongs to the hearth of neither.

When the green trolley finally clanged to the curb, its brakes squealing against wet iron, Eli stepped aboard with the rest of the silent crowd. He paid his nickel, took a seat near the rear window, and did not turn his head to look at the intersection as the car lurched forward into the dusk.

In his small apartment on Maple Street, he did not turn on the lamps. He walked directly to the bathroom, switched on the overhead tube, and leaned over the porcelain sink. The glass of the mirror was spotted with dried soap. In the harsh fluorescent glare, his face looked gray, the skin around his eyes pulled taut with the familiar, grinding strain of lipreading.

First came the shame of his cowardice: the cold, sick knowledge that he had watched an old man of his own blood get humiliated in the rain and had hidden behind a hearing scarf.

Then, immediately behind it, came the second shame, hotter and uglier: the shame at having felt ashamed. The bitter, cowardly realization that a part of him had been grateful to the boys, grateful for the reminder of what happened to anyone foolish enough to let their hands show, grateful that he could still put on his tie, step onto the train, and pass.

Eli reached up, turned off the light, and stood in the dark bathroom with his forehead pressed against the cold glass of the mirror, breathing the silence that was never silence, waiting for the trembling in his knees to stop.
