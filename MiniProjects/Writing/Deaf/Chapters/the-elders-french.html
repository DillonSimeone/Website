The Elder's French

The parish rectory behind St. Ann’s was smelled of dry paper, floor wax, and the dry, metallic radiator heat that made an old man’s knuckles ache. Jean-Luc sat in the high-backed wing chair by the sash window, his wool overcoat still buttoned to his chin despite the stuffiness of the room. Up close, the coat was even larger than Mateo had described: the padded shoulders dropped two inches past his clavicles, and the cuffs had been turned back twice to allow his wrists free movement.

Eli sat on a cane-bottom stool opposite him. He had been brought here by Mateo, who had slipped away to brew chicory tea in the scullery, leaving Eli alone with eighty-six years of memory.

The Elder did not sign in English word order. His grammar was old Langue des Signes Française—spatial, architectural, painting vast historical murals in three dimensions before collapsing them into a single flick of the fingers. Eli struggled to follow, catching perhaps forty percent through visual context and the residual French vocabulary he had learned in high school, but Jean-Luc adjusted with quiet grace, slowing his wrists, anchoring his subject signs with patient repetition.

He spoke of the Atlantic crossing. He had been a boy of eleven when the second delegation sailed from Le Havre. He signed the rolling of the clipper ship’s hull, the smell of salted herring and bilge water, the dizzying optimism of deaf men and women who believed they were journeying toward an unmapped continent where language would never again be tied to breath.

◈ ◈ ◈

“We arrived in Washington,” Jean-Luc signed, his hands tracing the Greek porticos of Gallaudet. “We were promised an acropolis. An academy where deaf thought would govern deaf destiny. And what did the Congress give us?”

He ticked the names off his left fingers: hearing presidents, one after another, century after century. Benevolent gentlemen with white muttonchops and doctorate degrees from Yale who addressed the students through hired interpreters, managing their budgets, selecting their curricula, deciding which signs were vulgar and which were polite.

“Whenever the students marched,” the Elder signed, his wrists making the sharp forward thrust of a protest banner, “the trustees came out onto the steps with baskets of bread and polite smiles. They said: Be patient. You are not ready yet. In twenty years, perhaps. In fifty years.”

Mid-sentence, the Elder stopped.

His hands, raised in the shape of a collective assembly, froze in the air between them. The fingers twitched once, then slowly dropped into his lap, coming to rest upon the rough gray wool of his coat.

His eyes shifted toward the radiator. His gaze became vacant, not clouded with confusion, but emptied, like a house whose shutters had suddenly blown shut in a squall. The room became completely silent. The only movement was the dust motes spinning in the shaft of afternoon sunlight from the window.

Eli did not move. He did not lean forward. He did not clear his throat. A younger man might have spoken, or tried to supply the missing sign, but Eli had spent his entire life in the margins of conversations; he knew how to wait in the dark. For sixty seconds, ninety seconds, two minutes, the old man remained motionless, breathing softly through his nose, his thin chest barely lifting the heavy fabric of the overcoat.

Then Jean-Luc’s eyes cleared. His gaze found Eli’s face again. He did not apologize. He did not acknowledge that he had wandered off the continent of thought. His right hand lifted effortlessly from his knee and resumed the sentence, not where it had fractured, but two miles downriver, arriving at the core with surgical weight.

◈ ◈ ◈

Jean-Luc looked directly into Eli’s eyes, his old fingers forming the sign for almost—the open hand brushing past the cheek, falling just short of contact.

“They always gave us almost. Almost our own school. Almost our own language. Almost our own world. Almost is how you keep someone leashed.”

The old man held the sign for three beats, letting the space between his palm and his cheek hover in the air. “If they gave you nothing, Eli, you would fight. You would burn their gates down with your bare hands. If they gave you everything, you would be free. So they give you eighty percent. They give you seventy percent. They give you a desk in the back of the ledger office and a gold watch after thirty years, and they tell you that you are an equal citizen—right up until the day they forget to write a note.”

Eli’s breath caught in his throat. He had not told Jean-Luc about Henderson. He had not mentioned the ledgers or Carter or the railroad cars routed into the wrong yard.

“You spent your life living on almost,” the Elder signed gently, his fingers folding back into the cuffs of his oversized coat. “You thought almost was an achievement. You thought if you tried ten percent harder, if you watched their lips ten percent faster, you could turn almost into enough. But almost has no end, boy. It is a treadmill built on hearing grease. It runs as long as your legs can move, and when your knees give out, it drops you into the gutter.”

Mateo returned carrying two steaming ceramic mugs of chicory tea on a tin tray. Jean-Luc took his mug with both hands, his knuckles white around the glaze, his shoulders hunched inside the massive yoke of his coat.

Eli took his cup and looked out the sash window at the brownstone chimneys across the alley. The word was inside him now, heavy as lead shot: almost. He had lived an almost-life, with an almost-voice, in an almost-city. And for the first time in his twenty-six years, he knew with absolute certainty that he would never accept almost again.
