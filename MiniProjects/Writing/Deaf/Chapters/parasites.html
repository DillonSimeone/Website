Parasites

The Musician · Hearing Opposition · Comfort Over Justice · Appropriation as Weapon

Scroll to read

The opposition coalition called itself the Committee for Commonwealth Integrity. They met in the oak-paneled ballroom of the Parker House in Boston, their roster including prominent philanthropists, two former commissioners of education, and coastal land speculators whose summer estates bordered the Chilmark bluffs. They were not Klansmen or violent reactionaries. They were cultivated, churchgoing citizens whose comfortable lives were premised on the quiet assumption that charity was the highest form of justice.

“We built them the finest residential asylums in New England,” their spokesman, an attorney named Fairbanks, declared from the podium before television cameras. “We funded speech pathology chairs at Harvard. We gave them education, shelter, and modern amplification. And now these separatist agitators claim an island that belongs to the taxpayers of Massachusetts? What more do they want?”

Then Fairbanks turned on an open-reel tape deck connected to the ballroom loudspeakers.

Across the microphones drifted Rowan’s commercial recording from 57th Street—the smoothed, synthesized suite Miller had released before the break, layered with lush strings and sweet harp glissandos designed to make deaf rhythm palatable for suburban drawing rooms. Beside the tape deck, Fairbanks held up Holbrook’s magazine review.

“Listen to this,” Fairbanks announced with theatrical satisfaction. “This young man was raised oral. He was educated in our schools. When he applied himself to our culture, he created beauty that was celebrated from New York to London. He did not demand an island. He integrated. The separatist movement is not an expression of deaf culture; it is an ideological contagion promoted by radicals who refuse to assimilate.”

◈ ◈ ◈

Rowan read the newspaper clipping in the ferry terminal at Woods Hole, waiting for the evening boat to Vineyard Haven. His equipment—three crates of transducers, amplifiers, and raw cable—sat stacked on a baggage dolly beside him.

He was not wounded by the attack. He was consumed by an icy, blinding fury.

Not because Fairbanks hated him. Hatred required an investment of passion, an acknowledgement of the opponent’s humanity. Fairbanks had not thought about Rowan for five seconds. He had walked into a record shop, purchased a commercial vinyl album pressed by Miller, and wielded it as a cudgel to deny sovereign land to two thousand deaf families. It was lazy. It was the absolute, insulting banality of hearing supremacy: taking the very artifacts produced under commercial coercion and holding them up as proof that the coercion was benevolent.

They took our stolen voices and cited them as proof that we loved our chains.

Rowan did not call the Boston press. He did not ask Claire to draft a clarification. He sat on the ferry bench with his notebook on his knee and drafted a formal declaration in sovereign ASL, translated into print by the independent editors of the Vineyard Gazette.

◈ ◈ ◈

“The recordings played in the Parker House ballroom belong to Miller’s corporate syndicate,” Rowan wrote, his pen biting deep into the fibrous paper. “They are the product of an interpreter speaking over my hands and an industry that marketed my biological condition to sell perfume and soap. They are not my music. My music is arriving on the evening steamer to Martha’s Vineyard. If the Commonwealth wishes to know what deaf frequency sounds like when it is free, they will have to cross the sound with bare feet.”

The hearing dailies refused to print the statement, dismissing it as extremist rhetoric from an ungrateful prodigy. But the deaf underground press carried it across seven states within forty-eight hours, mimeographed on yellow paper and distributed through union halls and church basements.

When the ferry docked at Vineyard Haven, eighty people were waiting on the pier under the oil lanterns. They did not carry microphones or tape recorders. They stepped forward and took the heavy crates of transducers from the baggage deck with their own hands, carrying them into the dunes of Chilmark.

Fairbanks had thought an old phonograph record could chain a people to an asylum. He had not understood that when you play a man’s stolen voice back to his people, you do not frighten them into submission; you give them the match to light the fire.
