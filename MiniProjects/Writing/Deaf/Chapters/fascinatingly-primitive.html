Fascinatingly Primitive

The Musician · Exploitation · The Review · The Machine Named

Scroll to read

The laminated tearsheet arrived on Miller’s teak credenza on Tuesday morning, flanked by two silver thermoses of espresso. The headline of the national cultural review ran across three columns in elegant Bodoni: The Instinctive Cadence: An Unheard Genius Discovers Fire.

Miller was beaming. He tapped the center paragraph with a manicured pinky nail, looking at Rowan with eyes so full of paternal triumph that a casual observer might have thought he had fathered the boy. “Read that, Rowan. Right there in the second column. That is the kind of pull-quote that sells out Carnegie Hall before the box office even prints the program.”

Rowan took the sheet. The words were set in crisp black ink:

“...what Rowan achieves in his percussive suites is not technical mastery in the European tradition, but something far older, more visceral, and fascinatingly primitive. Deprived of the civilizing discipline of the auditory register, his compositions rely entirely on primordial bodily instinct—a raw, unmediated spasm of rhythm that reminds the listener of music before language itself.”

Fascinatingly primitive. A compliment designed to devour everything it pretended to admire.

Rowan read the sentence three times. He felt neither anger nor immediate grief; what he felt was a sudden, freezing precision in his chest. For six years, he had worked twelve hours a day calculating physical resistance—measuring the damping factor of birch ply against high-tensile steel wire, calculating harmonic standing waves with slide rules and calipers. He had not composed from “primordial spasm.” He had composed through mathematical discipline so rigorous that his fingertips had lost sensation from the tuning pegs. But to the reviewer, and to the hearing audience that nodded along, none of that labor could possibly exist. If a deaf man made art, it could only be an accident of biology—an illiterate monkey stumbling into the rhythm of a storm.

◈ ◈ ◈

Two days later, an envelope with no return address was slid under the steel fire door of Rowan’s rehearsal space in Long Island City. Inside was a handwritten note on heavy rag cardstock and an unlabelled 7-inch lacquer disc.

“They are eating you,” the note said, written in a sharp, slanted hand in violet ink. “They did it to Mary in sixty-eight. They did it to the dancers in Boston. When they say ‘raw,’ they mean ‘unowned.’ Come to 14th Street on Thursday evening. Ask for Nadia at the loading dock. You don’t have to join us. You just have to see what a shield looks like.”

Rowan had thrown the card on his workbench and left it there for forty-eight hours. His immediate reflex was irritation. He had spent his entire life keeping deaf organizations at arm’s length. He did not consider himself part of a political bloc. He was a composer; he had earned his contract with Miller through sweat and merit, not through special pleading. To be told by some stranger that his success was merely an administrative harvest felt insulting, like an accusation of complicity.

Yet on Thursday afternoon, Miller summoned him back to 57th Street for what he described as a “strategic packaging conference.”

Around the boardroom table sat Miller, an advertising executive named Paulson, and a stage designer who wore a black turtleneck and thick tortoiseshell glasses. Claire was seated to Rowan’s left, her cardigan replaced by a tailored blazer, her hands poised to translate.

“Here’s the vision,” Paulson began, unrolling large charcoal sketches across the conference table. “We tour twelve cities. In the center of the stage, we build a soundproof glass cube. Rowan plays inside the cube. Completely sealed. The audience outside hears the sub-frequencies through arena bass bins, but we project live cardiograms of his chest and throat onto the glass in real time. We light him from beneath with strobe pulses. We call the tour: The Sound of the Silence.”

The stage designer leaned forward, eyes gleaming. “We can give him white linen robes. No shoes. Bare feet on the glass floor. The visual metaphor is total: the solitary soul trapped in perpetual quiet, pounding on the walls of his cage to communicate with the living.”

◈ ◈ ◈

Rowan watched Claire’s hands. She signed the words with smooth, unblinking efficiency: glass cube... strobe light... trapped in quiet...

Across the table, Miller was looking at him with eager expectancy, waiting for the grateful nod, the humble bow of the head that had sealed every meeting for four months. Paulson was tapping a gold pen against the sketches. The glass cube stood in the center of the drawing, a transparent terrarium with a small human figure kneeling on the floorboards, hands raised in supplication toward an unseen crowd.

The room seemed to shrink. The air conditioned hum of the 30th floor pressed against Rowan’s eardrums like stagnant water.

They did not want his music. They wanted his condition, mounted on a velvet board with brass pins.

“No,” Rowan said aloud.

The word was loud. Because he could not modulate its volume, it crashed across the boardroom table like dropped silverware. The pitch was uneven, harsh and unmusical—the voice of an oral student whose vocal cords had been forced into shapes they could not hear. Paulson flinched. The stage designer blinked. Claire’s hands froze mid-motion, caught between English syntax and physical shock.

Miller’s smile faltered, but only for a fraction of a second before the paternal mask settled back into place. “Rowan, buddy, don't worry about the logistics. We’ll have medical consultants on site. You won’t feel confined. It’s theater, kid. It’s what hearing audiences need to grasp what you’re doing.”

Rowan did not answer. He looked at Claire, who had cast her eyes down to her notepad. He looked at Miller, whose eyes carried for the first time the sharp, defensive warning of a patron whose dog had growled at the leash.

Rowan stood up, leaving the Bodoni review and the sketches of the glass cube on the polished mahogany. He walked out of the boardroom, through the carpeted corridors lined with gold records, and took the service elevator down to 57th Street.

In his pocket, his fingers found the crumpled note from Nadia. It was six o’clock. The rain on 14th Street was just beginning to turn to sleet.
