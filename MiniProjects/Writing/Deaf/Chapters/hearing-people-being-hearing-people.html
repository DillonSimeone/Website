Hearing People Being Hearing People

Eli · The Letter · The Gap That Stays · Clarity Without Anger

Scroll to read

The blue airmail envelope arrived at the Chilmark post office in late October, forwarded through three union post boxes in New Bedford. The handwriting on the envelope was unmistakable: his mother’s careful, looping Palmer script, the ink blotted slightly on the stamp corner.

Eli sat on the salt-bleached steps of the general store, looking out across the gray Atlantic marshes. He slit the envelope with his thumb. Inside were four pages of cream linen stationery, dense with writing. It was the longest letter his mother had ever written him.

She had been following the island news in the New York Times. She wrote about the garden in Queens—how the hydrangeas had frozen early, how his father was still working three days a week at the depot despite his lumbago. She wrote that she had seen a photograph of the Chilmark petition in the Sunday magazine.

“We were so proud to see your name in print, Eli,” she wrote in her neat, unhurried hand. “You always had such a clear mind for organization. Your father said just yesterday that you were always so determined, even when you were little and Dr. Ebbets had you practicing your vowels by the hour. We always knew you would find your way among people who appreciate you.”

◈ ◈ ◈

Eli read the pages through twice, the salt wind fluttering the edges of the linen paper.

There was no mention of the afternoon in Queens. There was no mention of the lace handkerchief pressed against her crying mouth, or his father’s bitter cry: I just didn't want you to suffer. There was no acknowledgment of the hearing aids he had laid down under the streetlamp, or the twenty-six years of lipreading strain that had aged his eyes before his youth was spent.

She had not omitted these things out of malice or cowardice. That was the quiet, permanent tragedy of it. She genuinely did not perceive that they were part of the story. To her, Eli had simply undertaken a spirited, slightly unconventional adventure, like a boy who had decided to try sheep farming in Vermont. She was reaching out with all the love she possessed, using the only instruments in her cabinet: maternal pride, domestic gossip, and the warm, suffocating assumption that everything in the end could be folded neatly back into the family album.

Hearing people being hearing people. Reaching across the chasm with hearing tools that landed slightly wrong every single time.

He was not angry. The anger had burned itself out in the Boston rain two months ago. What remained was a vast, unclouded clarity. The gap between them was not a feud. It was not a grievance to be litigated or a wound to be healed with an apology. It was the permanent geometry of their two worlds. They stood on opposite sides of a canyon that language could not bridge, and expecting her to understand the shape of the abyss was like asking a gull to describe the taste of loam.

◈ ◈ ◈

Eli folded the letter and walked back to his desk in the sail loft. He took a single sheet of plain typewriter paper and his fountain pen. He did not write about the haptic floor. He did not write about Rowan, or Jean-Luc, or the sovereign charter being argued before the federal magistrates in Edgartown.

He wrote three sentences:

“Dear Mother and Father,

I am well, and the work here on the island is keeping us busy through the autumn storms. I am glad you are following the news in the papers, and I hope Father’s back improves before the winter freezes the rails.

With love, Eli.”

He addressed the envelope, licked the two-cent stamp, and placed it in the outgoing mail sack by the door. This was not an exile. It was not a severance. It was the honest, final shape of their peace: a window through which they could look at each other across the salt water, waving their hands with love, while never pretending again that they shared the same air.
