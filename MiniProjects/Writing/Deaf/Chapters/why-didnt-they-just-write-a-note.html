Why Didn't They Just Write a Note

Eli · Fired · No ADA · The Question That Breaks Something Open

Scroll to read

The dismissal took four minutes. Henderson did not ask Eli to sit down, nor did he close the frosted glass door that looked out onto the clerical pool. He simply handed Eli a buff envelope containing two weeks of severance in cashier’s checks and a standard release of liability.

“We’re reorganizing the regional division, Eli,” Henderson’s mouth shaped the syllables with smooth, untroubled courtesy. “A matter of operational fluidity. Your record in filing has been exemplary, of course. We wish you every success in future endeavors.”

Henderson extended a dry, manicured hand. Eli took it, nodded with his customary deferential dip of the chin, and stepped back to his cubicle. There was no argument to be made. In this country, in this decade, an employer answered only to common law and the board of trustees; the statutory protections that existed in other nations had never been drafted here. A deaf clerk was retained by benevolence and dismissed by whim.

He packed his metal ruler, his mechanical pencils, and his porcelain coffee mug into a corrugated box, carried it past twenty coworkers whose faces remained glued to their ledger sheets, and rode the elevator down to the rain-washed avenue.

◈ ◈ ◈

For three weeks, Eli existed in a state of weightless suspension. Every morning at six-thirty, the internal clock drilled into him by years of corporate diligence woke his limbs. He would dress in his pressed trousers, tie his maroon silk tie, and sit at his kitchen table until nine o’clock, watching the grey morning light shift across the cracked linoleum. He was not desperate; he had twelve hundred dollars in the municipal savings bank. What hollowed him out was the absence of a reason.

He had never missed a day. His ledgers had never contained an arithmetic discrepancy. When Henderson had spoken, Eli had reconstructed sixty percent and deduced the rest with flawless probability.

Then, on a damp Tuesday afternoon, he encountered Carter outside the public library.

Carter had been the junior analyst in the adjacent stall—a pleasant, sandy-haired young man who occasionally borrowed Eli’s stapler. Seeing Eli, Carter hesitated, then smiled with an uncomfortable, guilty warmth and bought two paper cones of roasted peanuts from a street cart.

They stood together under the library portico. Eli watched Carter’s mouth with predatory focus.

“Look, Eli,” Carter said, his jaw working around a peanut, “everyone was sorry to see you go. But you know how it was toward the end. Henderson was losing his mind with the quarterly audit.”

Eli looked at him. “The audit?” he spoke aloud, his voice carefully controlled.

“Well, yeah,” Carter said, waving a hand. “Those three distribution memos back in August. Henderson told the whole floor on a Tuesday morning that the Philadelphia rail heads were rerouting through Camden. He announced it right by the water cooler while you were keypunching. And then you routed all the coal cars into the northern yard. It took the logistics crew two days to untangle it.”

Eli’s chest went cold. “He said it by the water cooler?”

“Yeah. And the phone transfers,” Carter added, entirely without cruelty, treating the details as casual historical fact. “Whenever the regional directors called from Pittsburgh, you never picked up line four. We all had to sprint across the aisle to catch your transfers. It wasn’t a big deal at first, but after six months... people get tired, you know? Henderson felt like he had to communicate everything twice. He said having to think about how to tell you things was slowing the whole department down.”

◈ ◈ ◈

Carter finished his peanuts, patted Eli’s forearm with well-meaning awkwardness, and walked back toward the commercial district. Eli did not move from the portico.

The wind blew wet oak leaves against his shins. Around him, the city pulsed with spoken coordination: taxi drivers leaning out of cabs shouting street names, vendors calling out prices, secretaries laughing as they crossed the intersection arm-in-arm. An entire empire functioning on vocal breath that dissolved in the air three inches from his ears.

Why didn't they just write a note?

The question rose in Eli’s throat not as a shout of rage, but as an unbearable, luminous bewilderment.

One square of yellow paper. A single sentence scribbled in ballpoint: Reroute Philadelphia to Camden. It would have taken Henderson five seconds. It would have required three inches of ink and a sliver of adhesive. Eli would have read it in one second, routed the trains flawlessly, and filed the confirmation before noon. It was not a grand sacrifice. It did not require an act of Congress or a restructure of the corporate hierarchy. It required only that a hearing man take a pen and write eight words upon a pad.

Yet Henderson had not written it. Carter had not written it. Not because they hated him. Not because they conspired against him. But because to them, writing a note was an interruption—an unnatural, tedious friction in the effortless stream of their vocal world. They preferred to fire a human being, to sever his livelihood and erase four years of loyal labor, rather than undergo the five-second inconvenience of placing pencil to paper.

Eli turned away from the avenue and began walking west. He did not take the subway. He walked for two hours through the cold rain, his eyes fixed on the pavement, carrying the question in his chest like an unexploded shell. For the first time in twenty-six years, the shame of passing was gone. In its place stood something entirely new, terrifying and cold: the absolute realization that no matter how perfectly he moved his lips, no matter how many hours he spent reconstructing their spoken vowels, they would never write the note.
