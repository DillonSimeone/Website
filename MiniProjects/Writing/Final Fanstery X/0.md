# Chapter 0: The In-Between, The Solder, and The Sieve

The pressure began as a high-frequency whistle behind the bridge of his nose, right between his eyes, where the bone meets the frontal sinus.

Before the sky over Zanarkand cracked open like bad porcelain, Tidus had been twenty-two years old and smelled predominantly of bearing grease, ozone, and the sour water of the primary cooling basins beneath the stadium. He was not a blitzball star. He did not possess the broad-shouldered arrogance of his father, nor the effortless charisma that drew stadium lights like moths to an arc lamp. 

He was the boy with bruised knuckles who spent eighteen hours a day in the catwalks beneath the hydro-sphere, adjusting the pneumatic valve timing so the suspended sphere of water didn't shear its seals and drown eight thousand spectators in sector four. He understood tolerances. He understood that if a hydraulic line hummed above four hundred hertz, the copper fitting was about to burst and take someone’s jaw with it.

Then his father returned. Or something that carried his father’s smell of salt and old iron arrived in the form of a mountain that breathed water and tore the clouds into ribbons.

Auron had found him in the lower machinery tunnels, grabbed him by the collar of his grease-stained coveralls, and hauled him out onto the crumbling highway. 

*"Come,"* Auron had said. His one visible eye had been flat, dead, devoid of anything resembling human terror.

*"The pressure differential,"* Tidus had yelled over the roar of the sky. He had been pointing back toward the stadium conduits. *"The atmospheric seal broke! The entire basin is going to cavitate—"*

*"Look,"* Auron commanded, turning him toward the rift.

The sky was not simply torn. It was being folded. 

Auron pulled him into the gravitational slipstream of the leviathan. And that was where the error occurred.

A human mind is designed to perceive three spatial axes and a single unidirectional arrow of thermodynamic decay. It is wired for calories, for the weight of a wrench, for the curve of a lover’s collarbone, for the duration of a breath held underwater.

When Sin dragged Tidus through the boundary layer between the Fayth’s collective dream and the cold physical reality of Spira, the envelope did not seal behind him. He fell through the *in-between*.

It was not a tunnel. It was not darkness.

It was an unshielded exposure to the planetary mechanics of a world operating on a stolen engine.

For a duration that could not be measured by seconds, Tidus did not merely see Spira; he was crushed by its raw source code. He perceived the gravitational constant of the planet not as an abstract number, but as a dense, suffocating sheet of standing waves. He saw how the atmospheric mass was circulated by pyrefly density gradients; he saw how the entire cycle of reincarnation was an inefficient, leaking closed-circuit loop driven by a dead summoner’s oscillating panic. He saw the mathematics of aeonic projection—how mass could be conjured from memory through a high-frequency spiritual carrier wave—and he saw the structural flaws in every valve, every plate, every joint of the ancient machina that lay rusting in the silt of a thousand-year-old graveyard.

The knowledge was not given to him. It was dumped into his skull like molten lead poured into a wooden cup.

---

When Tidus woke, he was vomiting sea water onto cold, carved stone.

The headache was so violent that his vision strobed in pulses of blind violet. Every heartbeat sent a spike of physical pain through his sphenoid bone, as if someone were driving a rusted railroad spike upward through the roof of his mouth. His skull felt swollen, fever-hot, bulging against the skin of his temples.

He was inside a drowned temple. Giant statues of weeping men and sea beasts loomed in the gloom.

He tried to stand, but his vestibular system was haywire; his brain was attempting to calculate the Coriolis force of the planet’s rotation and the harmonic tension of the stone arches above him simultaneously. Equations he had never learned were cycling through his consciousness in a deafening, overlapping roar:

`F = (G * m1 * m2) / r^2 // PHASE OFFSET: +0.0034 rad // REJECTION COEFF: NULL`  
`PYREFLY FLUX: 1.442e12 p/m^3 // CARRIER OSCILLATION: 11.2 MHz`  
`CRANIAL CAVITY SATURATION: 104.8% // THERMAL DISSIPATION: INSUFFICIENT`

*"Stop,"* he croaked, pressing both palms against his forehead until his fingernails tore into the skin. *"Shut up. Shut... down."*

The thoughts did not stop. They accelerated. His brain was attempting to process a continent’s worth of structural dynamics through ninety cubic centimeters of biological wetware. The tissue was cooking itself.

Then the lights appeared.

Two divers in heavy, brass-fitted neoprene suits broke through the dark water of the submerged nave, followed by the clanking, sputtering roar of an ancient submersible salvage craft.

---

The ship smelled of diesel, vinegar, and rusted iron.

They did not speak his language. The girl with the bright green spiral eyes and the golden scarf yelled at him in a guttural, consonant-heavy tongue:

*"Bhk dryd eduin! E druehr dryd uvyd dy y dyk!"*

Tidus could not understand her words, but he could hear the ship.

He could hear the catastrophic knocking in the vessel’s primary extraction dredge—a heavy, three-ton submersible pump mounted to the stern. It was a three-phase impeller pump from an ancient maritime utility model, probably six centuries old, running on improvised seals and filthy vegetable oil. The impeller was vibrating at eighty-two hertz; the housing was three millimeters out of true; within twenty minutes of operation, the main drive shaft would shear, the casing would crack, and high-pressure steam would scald everyone in the lower hold.

He didn't ask permission. He didn't speak.

Tidus shoved past two armed Al Bhed crewmen, grabbed a ninety-millimeter steel spanner from the deck, and dropped into the oily bilge beneath the dredge.

*"Hym! Fryd yna oui tuveh?!"* one of the crewmen shouted, leveling a spear-gun at his neck.

Tidus didn't look up. His hands moved with terrifying, autonomous precision. The screaming pressure in his head subsided—just a millimeter, just a fraction of an inch—the moment his fingers touched iron. His mind needed work. It needed throughput. It needed to dump calculation into physical reality, or his frontal lobe was going to hemorrhage.

He loosened the bypass collar three turns. He hammered the retaining wedge until the eccentric wobble aligned to within twelve microns. He stripped a frayed copper ground wire with his bare teeth, spit the insulation onto the iron deck, and twisted the lead directly into the secondary capacitor bank to bleed off the inductive surge.

He tightened the final lug, slammed the starter lever down, and stepped back.

The dredge purred. The violent knocking vanished, replaced by a smooth, low-frequency hum like a sleeping cat.

The Al Bhed crewmen stood frozen. The spear-gun slowly lowered.

Rikku stared at him, her eyes wide beneath her brass goggles.

*"...Oui dryk?"* she whispered.

---

The dive occurred four hours later.

They dropped the dredge into the deep trench off the shelf of the submerged temple, hunting for salvageable machina cores. Tidus was in the lower dive cage, tethered by an air hose, watching the silt swirl in the beam of the halogen lamp.

The attack was instantaneous.

A Sahagin—an abyssal variant, four meters long, scaled in black obsidian and crested with barbed dorsal spines—slammed into the dive cage from the dark water below. The impact crushed the outer brass mesh. 

A high-pressure steel drive gear on the dredge’s auxiliary winch snapped under the sudden hydraulic torque. The severed gear, fifty pounds of toothed cast iron spinning at two thousand revolutions per minute, ricocheted off the cage wall and sheared directly through Tidus’s chest.

It entered below his left clavicle and tore diagonally downward through his ribcage, exiting above his hip.

On the salvage deck above, Rikku screamed into the dive comm. Two crewmen hauled the cage up with emergency winches, expecting to haul up a shredded corpse in a cloud of crimson water.

They dragged Tidus onto the wooden planks of the deck.

The iron gear had torn a trench four inches deep across his torso. The chest cavity was open. Three ribs were cleanly severed. The pericardium should have been punctured; the descending aorta should have been severed; he should have bled out in six seconds.

There was no blood.

A clear, viscous fluid—faintly iridescent, smelling faintly of summer rain and high-voltage discharge—wept sluggishly from the margins of the wound. Beneath the pale skin, where muscle, fat, and bone marrow should have been, there were no cells.

There were no capillaries. There was no flesh.

There was only a blindingly dense, geometric lattice of microscopic light. Thousands of tiny, luminescent particulates—pyreflies—were suspended in a rigid, cohesive standing wave, holding the exact shape, density, and texture of human tissue.

Tidus lay on his back, staring up at the white sun of Spira. He was not in shock. His pulse did not race, because there was no heart beating against his ribs—only a harmonic oscillator pulsing in time with an unseen rhythm.

*"P-Puek?"* Rikku stammered, dropping to her knees. She reached out with trembling, grease-stained fingers, hovering above the gaping tear in his chest. *"Fryd... fryd yna oui?"*

Tidus didn't answer. He propped himself up on his elbows. He looked down into his own chest.

He reached inside the wound with his bare index finger.

He touched the severed end of his own fifth rib. It was not calcium and collagen. It was condensed, crystalline light, vibrating at forty-four kilohertz. He scraped a tiny flake of the luminous residue onto his thumbnail, stood up on unsteady legs, and walked past the paralyzed crew toward the salvage laboratory in the wheelhouse.

---

The ship’s microscope was a monocular brass instrument used by the Al Bhed to inspect micro-circuits and corrosion pitting on salvaged salvage wafers.

Tidus clamped the slide beneath the lens. He adjusted the coarse focus knob. He peered into the eyepiece.

At 400x magnification, human blood is a soup of biconcave red cells and defensive leukocytes.

Tidus’s sample was not blood.

It was an impossible fractal. Tiny, microscopic nodes of coherent pyreflies were arranged in a self-correcting crystalline grid. When he smeared a drop of seawater and dirty machine oil across the slide, there was no biological reaction. No lysis. No bacterial colonization. The pyreflies simply shifted position, enveloped the foreign carbon molecules, bonded them into the lattice, and continued their oscillation.

Sepsis was impossible.

Organ rejection was impossible.

He was not an organism. He was an instantiated simulation. A persistent holographic projection of condensed spiritual mass, manufactured by a server he could not see, designed to mimic the weight and vulnerability of a boy named Tidus.

He pulled back from the microscope.

He looked at the wound in his chest. The margins of the tear were already knitting together—not with scar tissue, but by the slow, magnetic re-alignment of light particles snapping back into grid formation.

And yet, his head still ached. The pressure was still boiling behind his eyes.

*"The meat is a lie,"* he whispered to the empty cabin. His voice was flat, dry, raspy. *"The meat is just a shell. But the processing bottleneck... the bottleneck is real."*

He looked around the laboratory.

On the workbench sat an open Al Bhed arithmetic unit—a brass-cased mechanical logic processor salvaged from a sunken merchant barge. It had thirty-two mechanical registers, an inductive ferrite core, and a sub-dermal transceiver coil designed to interface with diving telemetry helmets.

Tidus picked up a pair of surgical snips and a battery-powered soldering iron.

He did not cut open his skull. He was an engineer; he understood that you do not perform a full structural overhaul on an uncharacterized system. You begin with an instrumented test tap.

He wiped the skin behind his right mastoid bone with a rag soaked in mineral spirits. He took a piece of twisted copper wire—twenty-four gauge, enameled, salvage grade—and inserted it through the skin behind his ear, pushing the lead two centimeters inward until it brushed against the dense, buzzing light of his primary auditory nerve cluster.

He felt no pain. Only a sharp, cold prickle, like biting down on aluminum foil.

He soldered the exposed end of the copper wire to the inductive transceiver coil. He placed the coil flush against the bone behind his ear and sealed it in place with waterproof shellac.

He turned to the workbench. He energized the Al Bhed mechanical logic processor.

`INDUCTION TAP: ACTIVE`  
`EXTERNAL REGISTERS: 32`  
`CLOCK FREQUENCY: 1.2 kHz`

Tidus closed his eyes and directed the runaway calculation—the endless, maddening equations of Spira's gravitational shear—out through the copper lead, across the induction gap, and into the mechanical registers on the bench.

*Clack-clack-clack-clack-whirrrrr.*

The brass registers on the workbench began to spin furiously, spitting numbers onto a mechanical paper ribbon.

Inside his skull, the blinding violet light dimmed. The roaring pressure dropped by two pounds per square inch. For the first time since the sky over Zanarkand had shattered, Tidus took a breath that did not hurt.

He opened his eyes.

He looked at his hands—his pale, grease-stained, simulated hands.

*"It works,"* he murmured.

He looked at the open chassis of the ship's navigation computer. He looked at the spare copper coils in the bins. He looked at the vast, terrifying, broken world outside the porthole.

*"I need more wire."*
