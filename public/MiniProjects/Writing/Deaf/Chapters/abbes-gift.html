Abbé's Gift

Eli · History · Erasure · An Old Man's Slow Hands

Scroll to read

The diner on 4th Avenue was narrow, greased with the steam of frying onions and the constant percussion of ceramic plates on laminate counters. Eli sat by the frosted window, his fingertips wrapped around a cup of black coffee that had gone lukewarm. Opposite him sat Mateo, a young typesetter from the trade printing shop two blocks from Eli’s office. Mateo was twenty-four, with ink-stained cuticles and the restless, rapid signing style of someone whose hands had never been tied to a classroom chair.

They communicated in a hybrid, halting cadence. Eli spoke in low, carefully enunciated whispers while lipreading Mateo’s mouth, but Mateo’s hands kept spilling across the tabletop, punctuating words with sharp spatial flourishes that Eli could only partially interpret. It was an awkward, asymmetric encounter, yet Mateo had sought Eli out. Mateo had noticed Eli in the station newsagent two weeks earlier, watching him buy the evening paper with the tense, hyper-vigilant posture of someone trying not to be identified as deaf.

“You look like you’re waiting for an arrest,” Mateo had signed to him that afternoon on the platform. Eli had not answered then, but three days later, drawn by a curiosity he could not justify to himself, he had agreed to share a booth.

Now, Mateo was talking about the Elder.

“I went to the parish hall on Tenth Street last Tuesday,” Mateo signed, his fingers flashing above the sugar dispenser, his lips shaping the English words just enough for Eli’s eyes to track the sequence. “The old man spoke. Jean-Luc. Everyone calls him the Elder. He doesn’t like the title, but people use it anyway because there’s nobody left alive who remembers Paris before the second treaty.”

◈ ◈ ◈

Eli looked down into his coffee. “How old is he?”

“Eighty-four. Eighty-six. Somewhere in that country,” Mateo said, his left hand tapping the table in an easy, continuous rhythm. “He sat in an armchair by the radiator. He had an overcoat on—heavy dark wool with horn buttons. The coat was too large for him, Eli. Not just long in the sleeves, but wide in the yoke, hanging off his collarbones like it belonged to an older brother who had died in the war. He kept pulling the lapels together with his left hand, like a breeze was blowing right through the plaster.”

Mateo paused, his expression growing quiet, the nervous speed draining from his wrists. “When he speaks, it isn't like a speech. It’s an unraveling. He signs slow. Sometimes he stops right in the middle of a sentence, his fingers frozen in the shape of a verb, looking at the floorboards between his boots. The room stays completely silent. Nobody coughs. Nobody prompts him. You can see the minutes passing in his eyes—the way water takes its time to fill an empty cistern. Then his fingers unlock and he finishes the thought as if no time had elapsed at all.”

“What did he talk about?” Eli asked. He tried to keep his voice neutral, the measured voice of a corporate clerk examining an invoice for discrepancies.

“He talked about the Abbé,” Mateo said.

Eli knew the name from school, though only in the sanitary footnotes of his fourth-grade history textbook: Abbé Charles-Michel de l’Épée, French benefactor, founding father of deaf education, friend to the silent soul. In the classroom, Sister Catherine had pointed to a sepia engraving of a benign, white-haired priest in cassock and bands, surrounded by angelic deaf children looking up with moist, reverent eyes.

“The old man said the Abbé loved deaf people,” Mateo signed, his fingers curling with a sudden, bitter precision. “That was the poison of it. He loved us so much that he couldn't bear the thought of our souls languishing in pagan silence. When he saw two deaf sisters in Paris signing to each other with their mother’s dialect, he didn't see an indigenous grammar. He saw raw, unformed clay. He thought our hands were empty vessels waiting for Latin syntax to redeem them.”

“Like being handed someone else’s hands.”

Mateo repeated the phrase twice, his fingers mimicking the shape of wooden joints being forced into living wrists. “That was what the Elder called fingerspelling. A hearing person’s alphabet chopped into twenty-six static pictures, crammed into our knuckles so we could spell out their French, their English, their grammatical cases. He said it was like being given a wooden prosthetic and told it was an improvement on your own bone. Laurent Clerc brought it across the ocean on the ship with Gallaudet, carrying it like holy relics. Clerc meant well. Gallaudet meant well. The Abbé meant well. Every single one of them was trying to save us from damnation.”

Mateo leaned across the grease-filmed table, his eyes locking onto Eli’s with an intensity that made Eli want to slide deeper into the red vinyl booth. “And the result? Two hundred years of being told that our own hands are illiterate unless they are spelling out the vowels of a hearing throat. A hearing man’s architecture, built inside our palms, called a gift.”

◈ ◈ ◈

Eli did not answer. He stared at his own right hand resting on the table. His nails were clean, trimmed square. The thumb and index finger bore the faint calluses of a man who held a ballpoint pen eight hours a day, filling out distribution ledgers. For twenty-six years, those hands had lived in his pockets or folded neatly on top of desk blotters. Whenever he had used them to gesture in public, his mother’s voice had sounded in his head, gentle and firm: Hands down, Eli. Look at my lips. Use your words.

The waitress arrived and slapped the check face down between their cups. Mateo picked it up without looking, dropped two crumpled bills on the saucer, and stood. He pulled his leather cap over his thick hair.

“The Elder’s hands were shaking by the end of the evening,” Mateo said quietly, signing near his waist where the waitress could not see. “Not palsy. Just the cold. And the weight. He told us that whenever a hearing man offers you a gift with both hands open, you have to look at what’s tied to the wrists before you take it.”

Mateo tapped Eli’s shoulder twice—the brief, universal deaf contact—and turned toward the door, disappearing into the cold drizzle of 4th Avenue.

Eli sat alone in the booth for twenty minutes. The diner buzzed around him—the cashier ringing the register, the cook shouting orders over the hiss of the griddle, customers conversing in rapid, seamless arcs of sound that passed above his head like radio signals through empty air. He looked down at his fingers again. He curled them slowly: first the thumb tucked against the palm, then the four fingers pressing down over the knuckle. An A. The Abbé’s letter. The hearing man’s key to an open door that had never led outside.

He shoved his hands into the deep pockets of his mackintosh, paid for his coffee, and walked out into the rain.
