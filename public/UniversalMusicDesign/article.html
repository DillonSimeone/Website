Tactile Music Perception, Cochlear Implants, and Universal Multisensory Design

By Dillon Simeone, Deaf Lead Design Engineer Published: October 2026 Project: UMD NEA Project (25-014 NEA)

Introduction: The Lived Reality of Deaf Musical Experience

For generations, mainstream society has operated under the assumption that music belongs exclusively to the sense of hearing. When people imagine deafness, they often picture an absence of sound: silence, isolation, and an inability to experience musical art.

As a completely deaf engineer and musician, my reality is the exact opposite. Music is not merely an auditory pressure wave entering an ear canal. It is physical energy, kinetic motion, vibration traveling through flooring, tactile resonance in your chest, and visual rhythmic timing. When hearing is absent from birth, the human brain reorganizes through neuroplasticity: sensory pathways adapt to read tactile and visual patterns with remarkable sensitivity and nuance.

"Music is physical energy. When you remove acoustic bias, the body becomes an extraordinarily sensitive receiver for rhythm, transient strikes, and harmonic movement."

Why Cochlear Implants Struggle with Complex Music

Cochlear implants (CIs) are modern medical marvels designed to help deaf individuals understand human speech. However, what makes a cochlear implant effective for conversational speech is precisely what makes it struggle with music.

A standard cochlear implant uses an array of 16 to 22 tiny electrode nodes placed inside the inner ear. It replaces thousands of natural microscopic auditory hair cells with a handful of electronic contact points. To prioritize spoken words in noisy rooms, manufacturers build aggressive digital signal processing into the speech processor:

  • Speech and Noise Suppression Filters: Algorithms continuously listen for non-speech noise and turn it down. In music, drum beats, bass guitar hum, and cymbal shimmer are often mistaken for background noise and muffled or clipped.
  • Heavy Dynamic Compression: Speech processors squeeze wide acoustic dynamics into a narrow electrical range so whispers and shouts sound equally loud. This removes the dramatic impact, punch, and rise of musical performances.
  • Narrow Frequency Windows: The lowest frequencies of human pitch (sub-bass notes between 20 Hz and 100 Hz) fall below the physical frequency floor of typical cochlear electrode channels.

When a cochlear implant user listens to complex music, multiple instruments playing at once can collapse into a harsh, digital buzzing sensation. Subtle details, like acoustic drum doubling or natural cymbal decays, get swallowed by speech algorithms.

The Skin as a High-Bandwidth Sound Highway

Human skin contains specialized nerve endings called mechanoreceptors (such as Pacinian corpuscles) that are exceptionally responsive to vibrations between 10 Hz and 250 Hz. This frequency band directly overlaps with the fundamental frequencies of musical rhythm, basslines, kick drums, and physical grooves.

By placing precision vibrotactile transducers on the body (torso, wrists, or handheld units), we provide a direct physical bypass around the damaged ear. The skin feels the attack of the snare, the sustain of the bass guitar, and the pulse of the tempo with instant, zero-latency feedback.

The Universal Music Design (UMD) Breakthrough

Instead of choosing between cochlear implants and haptic wearables, our research pairs them together. We create custom "debloated" cochlear profiles (turning off aggressive speech filters, opening linear volume control, and widening input range) while simultaneous haptic transducers deliver physical punch and sub-bass rhythm to the body.

Directional Sound and Torso Spatialization

One of the greatest challenges for people with hearing loss or single-sided deafness is knowing where a sound came from in a room (sound localization).

During our recent laboratory testing and public workshops at Portland State University, we demonstrated stereo and multi-channel haptic translation. By sending independent left and right audio channels to opposite sides of a haptic vest, deaf users can immediately identify where an instrument or speaker is located in the room based on which side of their chest or back vibrates.

This spatial awareness creates a profound sense of presence and connection during live rehearsals, performances, and public events.

Democratizing Hardware: From \$1,000 Gear to \$10 Accessible Units

Currently, commercial haptic vests cost upwards of \$500 to \$1,000 each, and rely on bulky cables and analog radio transmitters that limit accessibility for schools, community clubs, and grassroots deaf artists (such as the UK Deaf Rave community).

Our active engineering work focuses on solving this bottleneck:

  1. Auracast Bluetooth Broadcast Audio: Upgrading our 60-unit rehearsal fleet to Bluetooth Low Energy Auracast. A single transmitter can broadcast clean, low-latency stereo sound to unlimited listeners simultaneously without messy wires.
  2. \$10 Off-the-Shelf Receivers: Bench-testing affordable plug-in modules from AliExpress that bring modern wireless haptic audio within reach of grassroots deaf organizations.
  3. Smartphone Haptics: Transforming everyday Android smartphones into high-performance haptic music monitors using custom open software, requiring zero specialized hardware purchases.

Conclusion: Designing for Everyone

Universal Music Design is not a compromise or a secondary accommodation. By designing music tools that convey emotion, rhythm, and space through touch and vision, we make music richer, clearer, and more expressive for everyone: deaf, hard-of-hearing, and hearing listeners alike.
